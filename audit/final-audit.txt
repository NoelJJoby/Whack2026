The Daily Grind is an AI office simulation: eight Claude-driven employees react to corporate news, gossip and pressure, and their behaviour moves a company's share price. LLMs write what agents say and propose what they do; deterministic, seeded rules decide every number.

What it is
A browser game where you watch, and poke, a small software company over one quarter. Eight employees each have a personality, a role and live stress, burnout, loyalty and trust levels. Every two in-game hours they choose what to do: work, overwork, slack off, complain, gossip, check a rumour, interview elsewhere or leak to the press. A CEO and an Ops manager run the business: they respond to client wins, outages and budget cuts, and assign work. The player injects news (true or false), sets company objectives and watches how the office, and the share price, react.

Architecture
[embedded content: architecture · browser, server, engine, Claude, journal]

The browser sends controls and receives one frame per tick over SSE. The engine calls Claude for words and weighted proposals, checks every reply, then decides with seeded rules and commits the tick to disk before streaming it.

The game loop: one tick
Everything happens in runTick (server/tick.ts). It clones the world, runs these steps in a fixed order, and returns the new world plus a record per agent. Same seed and inputs give the same result.

Arrive / clock. Advance two hours. At 09:00 everyone still employed is back in.
Apply news. Scripted scenario events and anything the player queued. True official news is announced by the CEO to whoever is on the open floor; rumours start with one person. Effects (stress, trust, workload) apply to all if the news is true.
Business phase (office hours). New business situations arrive on a seeded schedule; the CEO picks a response per case (with budget and deadline), then Ops assigns tasks to people with capacity.
Agent turns. For each employee present: build a context (traits, feelings in words, rumours heard and from whom, their tasks, what is bothering them, who just spoke to them), call Claude once, validate the JSON. All calls run in parallel.
Bias engine. Turn Claude's weighted proposals into one action per agent (next section). Validate emotion; open or reply to conversations.
Resolve. Apply each action: work advances tasks, gossip passes a (possibly distorted) claim, verify checks a claim, leak goes to the press.
Passive updates. Overtime adds stress and burnout; stress, burnout and trust drift; off-shift recovery at 23:00.
Quit check. Anyone over their personal burnout threshold resigns and walks out.
Presence. At 17:00 and after, people go home unless overworking or overloaded.
Price and scorecard. Recompute the share price from output and news; update morale, retention, trust.
Record. Per-agent record (action, target, line, thought, emotion, cause), events, price. Committed to the journal and streamed to the browser.
LLM calls per tick: one per employee present (up to 6), plus CEO and Ops in office hours. In rules mode, zero.

How agents decide
The core idea: Claude proposes, rules decide. LLMs are too sensible: told an agent is exhausted and furious, they still pick "work calmly". So Claude returns up to three weighted proposals plus the words; the bias engine (server/bias.ts) reweights them mechanically by the agent's traits and state, then draws one action with a seeded random number.

Steps inside decide():

Mask actions the agent cannot take (no colleague present means no complaining; no rumour held means no gossip; no task means no work).
Normalise Claude's weights, with a floor so every available action keeps a small chance.
Modify by personality and state (pressure pushes slacking, complaining and gossip; ambition and financial need push overwork; burnout and low loyalty push interviewing; low trust in management pushes complaining, verifying and leaking). Multipliers are clamped, then scaled by impulsivity.
Blend and sample one action from a seeded stream.
Settle the target and claim, falling back to sensible defaults if Claude named someone invalid.
Every decision is logged with Claude's proposals, each adjustment and the random draw, so any action can be explained and replayed.

Prompt safety. Agents never see internal ids, claim truth, numeric stats or other agents' traits. Feelings arrive as words in bands ("you're running on fumes"). Ids become short keys (c1, t1, k1) and are mapped back afterwards. Player-written text is marked untrusted data. Dedicated tests fail the build if a prompt leaks an id, a truth flag or an archetype name.

Emotion and causes. Each turn also returns an emotion from a fixed list of 10. A rules-only trigger system (server/triggers.ts) lists concrete reasons an agent might be upset: bad news hitting their department, a task due soon and behind, high stress, a colleague who complained at them or slacked while they overworked, a rumour they hold. Personality amplifies a trigger but never creates one. Angry or frustrated without a valid cause is downgraded to stressed or neutral. In a 16-tick test run across 3 seeds: 0 of 469 records showed anger without a cause.

Rumours and truth
Every piece of news is a claim with a hidden truth flag the agents never see (server/claims.ts). What spreads is belief, not truth.

Hearing. Belief in a claim starts from trust in the source: trust in management for official news, trust in that colleague for gossip.
Gossip. Passing a claim on creates a child claim. Depending on the teller's distortion trait, it grows in the retelling: "30% more pay" becomes "45%", "a third of the team" becomes "half", or a spin is added ("Management has known for weeks").
Verify. An agent can check a claim with a colleague; the result (confirmed or refuted) locks their belief and adjusts how much they trust that source.
Leak. An agent who believes a scandal can leak it to the press. That becomes public news and hits the share price; if it was false, the market corrects it after six ticks.
Reach. The engine tracks how far true and false claims have travelled (shown in the Rumour reach chart).
CEO announcements. The CEO is the single source of truth for official news (merger, new management, long-hours policy, bonus). A true official event makes the CEO walk to a town-hall spot and announce it. Only agents on the open floor that hour hear it first-hand; anyone in the lounge, visiting someone, outside or at a window hears it later through gossip, distortion included. Rumours (PR scandal, layoff rumour, competitor poaching) always start on the grapevine with one person, never the CEO. Toggle with ENABLE_ANNOUNCEMENTS=false.
